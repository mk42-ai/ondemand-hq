A selection of parts sourced from broken down vehicles is keeping this truck on the road. Tires, valves, cylinders, whatever can be salvaged. Across Gaza, drivers are trying to keep a shrinking fleet operational. Before the war, the Private Transport Association had 1,200 trucks to carry out deliveries. Today, only 300 are operational. Many have been damaged or destroyed by Israeli bombardment. Others need new tires, spare parts, or engine oil, which are hard to come by. The situation is catastrophic. Before the war, a tire cost around $300. Today, that same tire cost as much as $7,000. For transport companies, every trip has become a calculation. Find the part, find the fuel, get the truck on the road. For some drivers, the risks go far beyond the conditions of their vehicles. Nasarim says his son Ahmed was killed by Israeli forces while trying to bring aid into Gaza. He was at the Philadelphia corridor when the truck in front of him broke down. He signaled to a nearby Israeli soldier, indicated that he wanted help to repair the truck. The soldier did not respond. Ahmed got out of his truck and went to check the damage. The soldier drove towards him, pulled out his gun and shot Ahmed in the head. [snorts] The journey to get humanitarian aid into Gaza begins in Egypt. Trucks carrying supplies hit towards the Rafa crossing before being directed to the Karma Island crossing where they undergo Israeli inspection. From there, the trucks enter the strip and take one of the two main routes. One is along Salahedin road which is badly damaged. The other is Arashid road which runs along the coast. From there trucks travel to warehouses and distribution points across Gaza. The issue is not only how much aid enters Gaza but how much actually can be moved once [music] it gets in. With damaged road, fewer operational trucks and a shortage of basic supplies. The collapsing transport network is a major factor in this humanitarian crisis. Elazer from Aida town, Palestine.
